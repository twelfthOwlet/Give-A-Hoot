## Trial by Fire: Nested Hweets From SMT Benches to Robotic Welders

Long before Hollywood popularized it, "see a need, fill a need" was simply the unwritten job description of working maintenance in a lean industrial environment. At Western Reserve Controls, there were no rigid silos. If a machine went down, you fixed it. If a communication board died on a reflow oven, you didn't wait six weeks for an OEM service tech—you brought in an old PC, wired up a bridge, and made it the eyes of the thermal profile. When manual reel data entry bottlenecked production, you introduced a spreadsheet and a barcode scanning workflow to shoot component reports straight to the manager.

That was the baseline: walking into a room full of legacy hardware—from dual FCP II placers and a wave solder machine to orange-display terminals running vintage OS environments—and figuring out how to make them talk, how to modify Microwave OS9 code for the Fuji controllers, and how to keep the line moving without a safety net. You learn real electronics and systems-thinking when failure isn't an abstract error code, but a dead machine on a live floor.

## The Chocolate Factory Pitstop: You Can't Hweet Engineer Management

Not every shop wants to be fixed. If WRC was a trial by fire, Harry London's Candies was a lesson in bureaucratic quicksand. Instead of reverse-engineering systems or rebuilding legacy lines, the day-to-day devolved into mindless amp draws and basic motor voltage checks. 

When I took the initiative to build out a proper preventative maintenance program from scratch, management immediately shot it down. That’s the exact moment you know it’s time to pack the toolbox. It is always amusing how quickly a Maintenance Manager will suddenly find a carrot to dangle the second you tell them you're walking, but by then, the answer was an easy, "Nah." You can fix a broken communication board, but you can't engineer out bad management.

## Scaling Up the Hweets: Heavy Manufacturing & Robotics

That engineering mindset found a real home again in heavy manufacturing at East Mfg Corp. While the environment shifted from SMT benches to aluminum trailers and steel dumps, the core challenge remained the same. The custom welding machines survived on proprietary controllers managing wire speed, feed rates, and arc initiation. Those controllers—along with the notoriously frustrating guiding probes—became second nature.

When the facility expanded to house the new robotic welding lines, the role scaled up with it: helping bring the building online, learning the code required to program the welding bots, and running dry runs to iron out the bugs before the first live arc was ever struck.

## The Cyber Detour & The Hweeted SANS Scramble

There comes a point in heavy maintenance where you want to trade the steel and grease for a keyboard. By 2017, the goal was to pivot fully into IT, locking down the CompTIA trifecta. But the tech industry doesn't always open its doors just because you have the certs. 

In 2019, the push continued with a Bachelor's track in Cybersecurity and Information Systems at Columbia Southern University. But life doesn't respect a syllabus. A massive family disaster involving my father derailed the next four years, forcing everything into a holding pattern. 

When the dust finally settled, it was time to go all-in. Twenty thousand dollars and a grueling SANS Institute journey later, the resume was stacked with heavy-hitter certifications: GFACT, GSEC, GCIA, GCLD, and GCIH. It was enough firepower to finally resign from East Mfg and chase the cybersecurity dream full-time. 

And then... it fell through. The cyber pivot hit a brick wall. 

## Full Hweeted Circle: Automated Fulfillment & The Nocturnal Melt

When the grand plan stalls, you scramble and fall back on what you know: keeping complex systems alive. That scramble led to a fulfillment center, stepping in as a Digital Asset Technician (DAT) before the role rapidly mutated into an Automation Maintenance Technician (AMT). 

Along the way, the academic loose ends got tied up with an Associate of Science in Information Technology from CSU. But more importantly, the reality of the third-shift gig started to set in. This wasn't just turning wrenches on conveyors; this was high-level, software-driven industrial automation.

Suddenly, the "see a need, fill a need" instinct kicked right back in. Only this time, filling the need didn't mean hacking an old PC to save a reflow oven. It meant bridging 5-pin RS-485 stepper motor telemetry, tapping into UART diagnostic ports on automated guided bots, and diving into QNX command-line logs to trace system errors. 

The IT and cyber training didn't go to waste—it just weaponized the maintenance background. The tools changed, but the mission didn't.
